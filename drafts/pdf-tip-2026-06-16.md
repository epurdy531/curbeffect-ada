Document Accessibility Tip of the Day

📃Topic: Insert footnotes and endnotes with Word's References tools, not by hand📄

It is tempting to drop a tiny superscript number next to a word, scroll to the bottom of the page, type the same number, and call it a footnote. Visually it looks right. Structurally it is just two pieces of ordinary text that have no connection to each other, and that disconnect is exactly what carries through into the exported PDF.

In Word, place your cursor where the reference belongs and use References > Insert Footnote (or Insert Endnote). Word creates the superscript reference mark, drops you into the matching note area, and — most importantly — records the relationship between the two as document structure rather than as styled characters. Do the same for every note in the document, and let Word's automatic numbering handle renumbering when notes are added, moved, or deleted, so the marks and the notes never drift out of sync.

When you export with Save As PDF or Export and structure tags turned on, that real footnote relationship survives into the tagged PDF. A screen reader user can hear that a reference mark is present, follow it to the note, and return to where they were in the body text. With hand-typed superscripts and bottom-of-page text, none of that exists — the note reads as loose text inside the body flow, often in the wrong reading order, and remediating it later means rebuilding the link by hand in Acrobat for every single note.

The same rule applies to citations and cross-references: use References > Cross-reference and Word's citation tools instead of typing the label yourself. The few extra clicks at authoring time replace a slow, error-prone cleanup pass on the PDF.

🔍 Reference: for the exact normative wording, see PDF/UA clause 7.9 in your ISO 14289-1 copy.

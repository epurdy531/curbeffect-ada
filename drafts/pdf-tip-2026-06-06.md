Document Accessibility Tip of the Day

📃Topic: Making mathematical expressions readable in a tagged PDF📄

Math is one of the easiest things to get wrong in a remediated PDF. An equation that looks crisp on screen is often a jumble of disconnected glyphs, italic letters, and floating superscripts to a screen reader — "x squared plus two x" reads as "x two plus two x" because the exponent was never grouped, the variables were never identified as math, and the reading order skips around the expression. A little structural intent at tagging time turns that into something a listener can actually follow.

The core idea is that an equation is a single semantic unit, not a paragraph of stray characters. Wrap each expression in a Formula tag and give it an Alt attribute that spells the equation out in plain English the way a person would read it aloud — for example, "x squared plus two x equals y." That alt text is what assistive technology speaks, so it should be unambiguous, use words for operators where it helps comprehension ("equals", "plus", "divided by"), and match how the author would dictate the formula in a lecture. For longer derivations, tag each numbered line as its own Formula rather than rolling several steps into one block, so the reader can pause and re-read step by step.

If the equation was authored in Word with the Equation Editor, the export carries a Formula tag through automatically, but the alternative text is usually missing or mechanical — open the tag in Acrobat's Tags panel and write a human-readable Alt attribute yourself. If the math was rasterized to an image during export, retag the image as Formula (not Figure) and provide the same kind of spoken-language alt text; do not leave it as a decorative artifact, because then the equation simply vanishes from the reading experience. Inline math inside a sentence belongs in a Formula tag nested within the surrounding paragraph so the sentence still flows naturally when read aloud.

For documents with heavy math, plan for MathML in the long run — Acrobat now supports an associated MathML stream on a Formula tag, and that gives assistive technology a true semantic representation rather than a single string of words. Until that is part of your toolchain, a thoughtful Alt attribute on every Formula tag is what stands between an equation and a listener who would otherwise hear nonsense.

🔍 Reference: for the exact normative wording, see PDF/UA clause 7.7 in your ISO 14289-1 copy.
